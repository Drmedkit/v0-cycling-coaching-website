Two riders can complete the same route at the same power and experience a different training dose. One can also repeat the same workout two weeks apart and find that it creates a different response.

This is why training load is more useful when separated into **external load**—the work performed—and **internal load**—the individual response to that work.

The distinction was formalised to make the training process clearer, not to create two competing scores. Impellizzeri, Marcora and Coutts describe external load as the physical work prescribed or completed, and internal load as the psychophysiological response that mediates adaptation ([Impellizzeri et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30614348/)).

## External load: what you did

On the bike, external-load information can include:

- duration and distance;
- power output and work in kilojoules;
- time in intensity domains;
- climbing, cadence and torque demands;
- interval repetitions and recoveries.

It makes the session concrete. “Four by eight minutes at 290 watts” is a clearer exposure than “a hard ride.” External load is also the side a coach can prescribe most directly.

But it cannot tell us the full dose received. A power meter sees the crank, not the athlete’s sleep, heat strain or developing illness.

## Internal load: how you responded

Internal-load information can include heart rate, blood lactate in a laboratory setting, oxygen consumption, session rating of perceived exertion and subjective signs of fatigue or readiness.

For everyday coaching, heart rate and perceived exertion are powerful because they are repeatable and available. Neither is perfect. Heart rate changes with temperature, hydration, caffeine and cardiac drift. Perceived exertion is subjective—but subjective does not mean meaningless. It is the athlete’s integrated experience of the work.

The question is not whether one signal is pure. It is whether several imperfect signals tell a coherent story.

## Read the relationship, not isolated numbers

Imagine a steady endurance ride at familiar power:

- If heart rate and perceived effort are both normal, the session likely cost what we expected.
- If heart rate is elevated in heat but effort remains controlled, environment may explain much of the difference.
- If heart rate is suppressed while effort is unusually high, accumulated fatigue or illness may deserve attention.
- If the same power becomes easier across comparable conditions, fitness may be improving.

None of these observations proves a diagnosis. They create a reason to ask a better question and to inspect the pattern over time.

## Why one load score is not the answer

Training platforms compress duration and intensity into convenient scores. These can help summarise a week, but the precision of the decimal place should not be confused with precision about adaptation.

Different sessions with a similar score can create different physiological and mechanical demands. A long endurance ride, repeated short sprints and sustained threshold work are not interchangeable because their totals look alike.

Load also does not automatically predict injury or readiness. Later work on the concept warns against treating load metrics and ratios as causal shortcuts without considering measurement and context ([Impellizzeri et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37022589/)).

## A practical post-ride note

You do not need twenty wellness sliders. A useful routine can be:

1. Did I complete the intended work?
2. How hard did the whole session feel from 1–10?
3. Was anything notably different—sleep, heat, stress, pain or fuelling?
4. How do I feel the next morning?

That short context turns a file of watts into coachable information.

External load tells us what stimulus was applied. Internal load helps reveal the dose your system experienced. Progress comes from managing the relationship between them—not choosing one dashboard as the truth.
