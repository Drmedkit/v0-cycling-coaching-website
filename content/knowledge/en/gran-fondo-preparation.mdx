A gran fondo is rarely decided by how strong you feel in the opening hour. The decisive question is what remains after several hours of climbing, cornering, eating, concentrating and responding to other riders.

Good preparation therefore starts with the event’s demands and works backwards. Distance matters, but so do elevation, climb duration, altitude, likely temperature, road surface, aid stations and the amount of riding you expect to do in a group.

## Build an event profile

Write down the practical demands before writing intervals:

- expected completion time and total climbing;
- number and duration of major climbs;
- steep sections that require low-cadence torque;
- long descents or technical roads;
- likely heat, cold or altitude;
- distance between reliable food and water points;
- whether the final hour contains a key climb or fast group finish.

This profile prevents preparation becoming a collection of generic “hard” sessions. A six-hour mountain event and a fast rolling fondo may share distance while asking very different questions.

## Develop the engine, then its durability

Early preparation can focus on general aerobic development, sustainable power and the capacity above it. As the event approaches, the work should increasingly resemble the demands you identified.

Durability is the ability to preserve useful power and efficiency after substantial prior work. It is trained by progressing long rides carefully and placing selected work later in those rides—not by turning every weekend into a race.

Examples can include a steady climb after two or three hours, controlled tempo blocks late in an endurance ride, or practising target-event pacing with accumulated work in the legs. These sessions are demanding and need recovery. Their value comes from specificity, not frequency.

## Pace below your excitement

The start of a gran fondo creates free motivation and expensive decisions. Groups move quickly, climbs feel easy and riders spend energy protecting a position that may not matter later.

Use power, heart rate and perceived effort as guardrails rather than absolute commands. Short rises above target may be tactically necessary, but repeatedly following surges can drain finite work capacity and increase fuelling needs.

A good pacing plan includes permission to let the wrong group go. The fastest first hour is irrelevant if it creates the slowest final two.

## Train the fuelling system

Fuelling is a behaviour as well as a nutritional calculation. You need to carry, open, chew and tolerate food while riding hard, in weather, around other people.

Use long rides to test:

1. what you can eat and drink per hour;
2. which products remain appealing after several hours;
3. how temperature changes fluid needs;
4. where bottles and food will be stored;
5. which aid stations are part of the plan and which are backup.

Do not introduce an ambitious fuelling strategy on event day. Individual needs and gastrointestinal tolerance vary; a qualified sports dietitian is the right professional for clinical or highly specific nutrition support.

## Practise the non-physiological skills

Descending, group positioning, clothing choices, tyre pressure and simple repairs all affect performance. So does staying calm when a bottle is dropped or a group disappears.

Include rides that use the equipment intended for the event. Test gearing on comparable gradients. Learn whether your rain layer can be reached without emptying every pocket. Confidence comes from removing small unknowns.

## Taper without going flat

The final weeks are not the time to prove fitness. Reduce fatigue while retaining enough intensity to keep the body familiar with race demands.

A systematic review and meta-analysis in endurance athletes found performance benefits from tapers that reduce volume while maintaining intensity and frequency, although the right duration and reduction still depend on the preceding load and athlete ([Wang et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/)). The practical lesson is not to copy one percentage. It is to arrive fresher without replacing training with complete inactivity.

## A useful final-week checklist

- Equipment inspected and gearing confirmed.
- Route, start logistics and aid stations understood.
- Fuelling packed by hour, with a small margin.
- No last-minute fitness tests.
- Sleep protected across several nights, not only the night before.
- Pacing decisions written while calm.

The best gran fondo plan develops more than peak power. It builds the ability to express your fitness after hours of work and the judgement to spend that fitness where it matters.
