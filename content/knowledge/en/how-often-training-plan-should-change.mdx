“Personalised” is sometimes presented as a calendar that changes every day. Constant movement can look attentive, but it can also erase the consistency required to learn anything from training.

A useful plan changes at different speeds. The season has a stable direction. Training blocks have a clear purpose. Individual sessions remain adjustable when the athlete’s response or life makes the original choice less useful.

## Keep the purpose stable

A block should usually last long enough to repeat a stimulus, recover and observe a trend. If the target adaptation changes after every disappointing session, neither athlete nor coach can tell whether the work was effective.

Stable does not mean identical. Interval duration, repetitions or total volume can progress while the underlying purpose remains the same. Endurance volume can build across several weeks without rewriting the season.

This is one reason testing should not trigger panic. A single result is a data point. It becomes more persuasive when it agrees with training performance, subjective response and repeated observations.

## Review the week regularly

For many ambitious amateur riders, a weekly review is a useful rhythm. It is frequent enough to account for work schedules, fatigue and completed training, but long enough to avoid reacting to every fluctuation.

A weekly review asks:

- What work was completed as intended?
- How did key sessions feel and progress?
- Is fatigue resolving between demanding days?
- What constraints are coming next week?
- Does the current block still serve the target event?

The answer may be “keep the plan.” That is an active coaching decision, not neglect.

## Change the plan when the assumptions change

Some events deserve an immediate adjustment:

- illness or symptoms that make training inappropriate;
- pain that changes movement or worsens during riding;
- a major loss of sleep or unusual fatigue;
- travel, work or family changes that remove the planned slot;
- weather or road conditions that make the session unsafe;
- a target event that moves or changes character.

The adjustment should preserve the larger aim where possible. Moving intervals away from a sleep-deprived day is different from abandoning the entire development block.

Concerning symptoms or injury questions need an appropriate healthcare professional. A coach can change training; a coach should not pretend to diagnose through a comment box.

## Do not change the plan for noise

Not every low number is meaningful. Power meters misread. Routes differ. Wind changes speed. Motivation fluctuates. Hard training sometimes feels hard.

Before changing direction, check whether the observation is repeatable and whether other signals agree. One poor interval after a stressful day may require compassion, food and sleep—not a new threshold test.

Likewise, one exceptional ride does not automatically justify adding load. Fitness improves through absorbed work, not through chasing every peak number with a harder week.

## Monthly and block-level decisions

At the end of a block, zoom out. Review changes in the power-duration profile, execution of key work, tolerance of volume, freshness and relevance to the next phase. This is where larger changes belong: a different emphasis, new testing, more specificity or a recovery period.

Across a season, target events set the broad sequence. The plan moves from general development toward event-specific demands and then toward freshness. Research in trained cyclists does not identify one universally superior periodisation model, which supports choosing a structure appropriate to the rider and context rather than following a fashionable template ([Galán-Rioja et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36640771/)).

## The simple rule

Change a session when new information makes it less useful or less appropriate. Change a block when the evidence says its purpose has been achieved, has stalled or is no longer relevant. Change the season when the goal changes.

Personal coaching is not measured by the number of edits. It is measured by the quality of the decisions those edits represent.
