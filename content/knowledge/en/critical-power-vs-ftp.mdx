Cyclists often treat Functional Threshold Power and Critical Power as two labels for the same physiological line. They may land near each other, but they are produced differently and describe performance through different models.

That is not an argument for deleting FTP from your head unit. It is an argument for knowing which question your test actually answered.

## FTP in practical terms

FTP is commonly estimated from a maximal 20-minute effort, with 95% of the average power used as the result. Other protocols and software estimates also exist. Its popularity is understandable: the test is familiar, easy to repeat and gives one number that can anchor training zones.

But the familiar shortcut contains assumptions. The percentage relationship between a rider’s 20-minute and longer-duration power is not identical for everyone. Pacing, motivation, anaerobic contribution and test familiarity all influence the result.

Research comparing FTP with laboratory thresholds has found strong group-level relationships alongside limits of agreement that are too wide to treat the measures as identical for an individual. One study in trained cyclists concluded that FTP was not equivalent to several blood-lactate parameters despite strong correlations ([Valenzuela et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31269000/)).

## Critical Power describes a curve

Critical Power is estimated from multiple maximal efforts of different durations. Together they describe the severe-intensity part of your power-duration relationship.

The model usually produces two useful parameters:

- **Critical Power (CP):** an estimate of the boundary separating exercise that can stabilise from work that drives progressively toward exhaustion;
- **W′ (W-prime):** a finite amount of work available above CP within the model.

The concept is a framework for exercise tolerance, not merely a replacement threshold number. The foundational review by Jones and colleagues explains how CP and W′ connect power, fatigue and the limits of severe exercise ([Jones et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20195180/)).

## Why the numbers sometimes look alike

Both measures sit in the neighbourhood of prolonged hard cycling, so a strong correlation is unsurprising. Correlation, however, does not guarantee agreement.

In a direct comparison of trained cyclists and triathletes, CP was on average slightly higher than FTP, but the individual limits of agreement ranged widely enough for the authors to caution against using them interchangeably ([Karsten et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33551839/)). For one rider the difference may be trivial; for another it may meaningfully change a workout prescription.

## Testing quality matters more than the label

A model is only as useful as its inputs. For Critical Power, the maximal efforts must cover suitable durations, be paced well and genuinely represent the rider’s current ability. A weak three-minute effort or an over-conservative long effort can distort both CP and W′.

FTP testing has similar practical requirements. Fatigue, cooling, nutrition and protocol changes can create a “new FTP” without a new athlete.

For useful comparisons:

1. Repeat the same protocol and conditions where possible.
2. Arrive sufficiently recovered.
3. Record context, not only the final wattage.
4. Look at the wider power-duration curve and recent training.
5. Avoid rebuilding every zone around one surprising test.

## How I use the information

Critical Power is valuable because it keeps the relationship between duration and power visible. It helps frame severe intervals, work above the boundary and changes in the athlete’s profile. FTP can still be a convenient communication and platform setting when its origin and uncertainty are understood.

Neither number should operate alone. Heart-rate response, perceived exertion, durability after accumulated work and the actual demands of the target event add information the threshold cannot carry.

The practical question is not “Which acronym wins?” It is: **which model helps us prescribe, observe and adjust this rider’s training most clearly?**

Numbers earn their place by improving decisions. When they stop doing that, the test has become a ritual rather than a tool.
